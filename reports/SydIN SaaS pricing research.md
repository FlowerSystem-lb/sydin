# Keep SydIN's $9 price, sell yearly

**What to charge (the short answer)**

1. **Lebanon:** Standard **$9 a month or $90 a year**. Pro **$19 a month or $190 a year**. Always show the yearly price first.
2. **Outside Lebanon** (Lebanese-run shops in the UAE, or anyone abroad who pays by Whish or USDT): **the same prices**: $9 / $90 and $19 / $190. No special country prices this year.
3. **Your first 20 paying shops (Founding 20):** **$69 a year** (Standard) or **$149 a year** (Pro), paid in advance.
4. **A shop that cannot pay a full year:** 6 months for **$49** (Standard) or **$99** (Pro).
5. **Optional setup visit:** **$29–$49**, paid once.
6. **Free plan:** stays free (50 items). A new shop can ask you on WhatsApp for 30 days of Standard free.
7. **Shops bigger than Pro:** keep the "Contact us" button. Do not publish a Business price yet.

SydIN should launch with the prices already in its code: **Free, Standard at $9 a month, and Pro at $19 a month, in US dollars**. What should change is *how* shops are asked to pay. Only three cloud tools sold in Lebanon publish a price on their own sites, and the cheapest is **$15 a month** (one directory lists a POPs restaurant app from $8 per user, but it is unclear whether that applies in Lebanon). Global rivals charge **$20–$49** for their first paid plan (BoxHero's $20 plan holds 1,000 items, like SydIN Pro). So $9 is not too high. Odoo's inventory app is free, but in Lebanon it is usually sold and set up through a paid partner, so SydIN competes with Odoo on simplicity, not on price. The real obstacles are different. The software offer Lebanese shops see most is a one-time desktop program sold for about **$100–$150** on OLX; some may be unlicensed copies (69% of software in Lebanon was unlicensed in 2017, the latest figure found). No survey measures what shops prefer. Most shops pay in cash. And the 2026 war is shrinking the economy. So the main advice is about payment shape, not the price number: make the **yearly plan ($90 or $190) the main offer**, open a capped **"Founding 20" deal at $69 or $149 a year**, give any new shop that asks **30 days of Standard for free**, and add a small **one-time setup fee**. Outside Lebanon, charge Lebanese-run shops in the Gulf the same dollar prices now. Do not offer special prices for Iraq, Egypt, Jordan or any other country this year. A middle guess, counted from the day the live site opens (not from today), is **about 15 paying shops by month 12, about $110 a month of subscription money** (monthly recurring revenue), and **about $1,300 collected** before hosting costs. A weak year gives about $450; a strong year about $3,600. This is a model, not a forecast. SydIN has no paying customers yet, so treat these numbers as targets to check against, not promises. They depend mostly on how many shops you visit and set up yourself. They also rest on US and European benchmarks, because no study measures what Lebanese shops will pay for software.

## In Lebanon, $9 is below every published local cloud price; the real rival is a $150 one-time program

Lebanon's market has three layers. At the bottom are one-time desktop programs sold on OLX for **$100–$150, "negotiable"** ([OLX Lebanon](https://www.olx.com.lb/en/services/q-accounting/)), and hardware bundles such as Hamster POS's "full package" for **$599** ([Hamster POS](https://www.facebook.com/HamsterPosLB/)). (POS, "point of sale", means checkout software.) In the middle are Lebanese firms that sell a **lifetime license** — pay once, keep forever — and give prices only on request ([POPs LEB](https://popsleb.com/faq)). On top is a newer group of cloud subscriptions: YellowPOS from **$15 a month** ([YellowPOS](https://yellow-pos.com/blog/best-pos-system-lebanon-2026)), Managely from **$35** on its Lebanon page (other pages on its own site reportedly start at $99) ([Managely](https://managely.cloud/en/best-pos-software-in-lebanon)), and MAPOS at **$49 or $99** (its own blog gives both) ([MAPOS](https://www.mapos.tech/en/blog/best-erp-software-lebanon-2025)). A Beirut agency tells small firms to budget **$20–$90 a month** for accounting software ([Voxire](https://voxire.com/blog/accounting-software-lebanon-smes-2026/)). SydIN Standard at $9 is **cheaper than every Lebanese cloud price with a published Lebanon figure**, and Pro at $19 is close to YellowPOS's entry price. The price level is not the problem.

The problem is habit. Lebanon's rate of unlicensed (copied) software was **69% in 2017**, the latest figure found (BSA, via [Byblos Bank](https://www.byblosbank.com/common/economic-research-new/lebanon-this-week/lebanon-this-week-540/lebanons-piracy-rate-at-69-in-2017-37th-highest-globally-and-sixth-highest-among-arab-countries)). Only **20% of small firms** invested in going digital during the crisis ([ResearchGate study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387556425_THE_IMPACT_OF_LEBANON'S_ECONOMIC_COLLAPSE_ON_COMMERCIAL_SMES)). Only **21.8% of adults** made or received a digital payment in 2024, and "habit" was the top reason for paying cash ([SSRN Findex analysis](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7488798)). For a phone-accessory shop, the real competitor is a paper notebook, WhatsApp photos, or Excel. And the software price it most likely knows is "about $150, once." A monthly fee invites the question "why pay forever?"

The answer is to sell the year, not the month. **$90 covers a whole year**, which is less than a one-time OLX program costs, and SydIN stays updated and backed up. Over several years SydIN costs more, so sell the updates, the phone scanner and the backups, not the price alone. A "first-year pack" of yearly Standard plus setup (about $119–$139) lands inside the $100–$150 range shop owners already accept for software, while SydIN still renews every year. This is my reading of the price evidence; no survey has tested it.

$9 a month also fits everyday Lebanese prices. It is **about 2.9% of the $312 monthly minimum wage** ([WageIndicator](https://wageindicator.org/work/minimum-wage/updates/2025/minimum-wage-updated-in-lebanon-from-01-august-2025-august-08-2025/)). It is close to Netflix Standard in Lebanon at $7.99 (Feb 2023 price) ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2255321)) or a basic Ogero internet line at about $7 (Sept 2023 tariff, the latest found) ([SMEX](https://smex.org/ogero-enacts-increases-in-fixed-internet-subscription-rates-in-lebanon-exploring-sustainable-solutions/)). It is less than one day's pay for a shop worker, who usually earns **$313–$500 a month** ([leb.business](https://leb.business/en/article/lebanon-salary-report-2026-what-workers-earn-by-sector-and-role)). "Less than one day of a worker's wage" is probably a stronger sales line than any comparison with Sortly or Zoho, but test it in real conversations. Pricing in dollars is also right. About **46% of GDP was a cash-dollar economy in 2022** ([Al Arabiya, on World Bank data](https://english.alarabiya.net/News/middle-east/2023/05/17/Lebanon-economy-now-increasingly-cash-based-over-lack-of-trust-over-banks-World-Bank)), the pound has held at **89,500 per dollar since mid-2023** ([CEIC](https://www.ceicdata.com/en/indicator/lebanon/exchange-rate-against-usd)), and every local vendor found prices in USD.

Collection also favours yearly. Whish wallet-to-wallet transfers are **free**, cashing out costs **1%**, a payment link costs **3%**, and a personal account holds at most **$3,000** ([Whish fees](https://www.whish.money/fees-commissions)); a Whish business account holds up to $30,000 ([leb.business](https://leb.business/en/article/whish-money-vs-omt-which-pays-better-for-lebanese-merchants)). Yearly payments can arrive close together, so cash out often, or open a Whish Business account before your wallet passes about $2,500. Sending $9 by OMT costs the sender about **LBP 50,000 (about $0.56)** ([OMT](https://omt.com.lb/en/services/money-transfer/omt-intra-local-money-transfer/price-list)). The research found **no automatic monthly debit** on Whish or OMT. So a monthly customer must remember to send money 12 times a year, and you must check and activate each payment. A yearly customer does it once. OMT has run "Cash to Business" schemes where members of a professional order pay yearly fees at any OMT branch ([OMT](https://omt.com.lb/en/news/cash-to-business-order-of-engineers-and-architects-in-tripoli)); ask OMT about this once you have dozens of customers. Cash is fine too. A shop can pay cash at any Whish or OMT agent into your number, or you can take the yearly fee in cash when you visit for setup and record it in /admin with the method "Cash". The receipt email then confirms the payment to the shop.

Three more Lebanese facts shape the plan. First, local rivals advertise **USD and LBP together and offline use during power cuts** ([AR POS](https://poslebanon.co/); [Dokani](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.dokani.app&hl=en_US)), so have a clear answer ready on both. The honest answers: **LBP — yes.** SydIN can show prices in LBP and issue an invoice in LBP while your prices stay in USD (Settings). **Offline — no.** SydIN needs Wi-Fi or mobile data, and with no signal it shows "You are offline". Say this plainly and never claim offline use. If many shops refuse because of it, note it as a reason. Second, SydIN's app is in English only. Many Lebanese owners read English, but some staff will not. In your first 50 trials, note every shop that says no because of language. If it is more than about 1 in 5, Arabic matters more for Lebanon than any price change. Third, timing is the biggest risk. The World Bank now expects Lebanon's economy to **shrink 6.4% in 2026**, with **17.5% inflation** and **1.2 million people displaced** after the March 2026 escalation ([World Bank, Aug 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/08/21/renewed-conflict-derails-lebanon-s-fragile-economic-recovery)). After the 2024 war, **15% of surveyed businesses closed for good** ([UNDP](https://www.undp.org/sites/g/files/zskgke326/files/2025-07/policy_brief_socioeconomic-impacts-lebanon-2024-war.pdf)). Do not raise prices in Lebanon in 2026, and expect slow paid sign-ups until the situation improves.

Finally, the size of the prize is small. The most-quoted count is **"over 225,000"** small and medium businesses, from an undated study that is probably pre-crisis ([IRC & Building Markets](https://www.rescue.org/sites/default/files/document/656/ircandbuildingmarketssmemarketoverviewlebanonrelease.pdf)). Nobody has counted phone-accessory shops or depots. If about 3,000 Lebanese shops are a real fit (my guess) and SydIN won 1 in 10, that is 300 shops and roughly $3,000 a month. Lebanon is the place to prove SydIN works, not the place it will earn most of its money.

## Global rivals charge two to five times more per plan, and the closest ones give phone scanning away free

Global tools show the ceiling. Sortly, the closest "visual inventory" rival, charges **$49 a month for 500 items and 2 users**, or $24 a month on a yearly plan in the first year only, under a 50%-off promotion that may be temporary; Sortly's help centre gives the standing yearly discount as 20% (about $39 a month) ([Sortly](https://www.sortly.com/pricing/)). BoxHero charges **$20 a month ($18 on yearly) for 1,000 items, 3 locations and 3 team members** ([BoxHero](https://www.boxhero.io/docs/faq/billing)). Zoho Inventory's Standard plan is **$39 a month, or $29 on yearly** ([Zoho](https://www.zoho.com/us/inventory/pricing/)). Loyverse's inventory add-on costs **$25 per store** ([Loyverse](https://loyverse.com/advanced-inventory)). Among paid plans at US list prices, only Inventory Now, a single-user device app, is cheaper than SydIN, at $5–$7 ([Inventory Now](https://www.inventorynowapp.com/pricing)). Free options (Odoo's one-app Inventory, Zoho Free with 50 orders a month, Stockpile) and Odoo's Lebanon price of about $7.25–$8.95 per user also undercut SydIN (see below) ([Odoo](https://www.odoo.com/pricing); [Zoho](https://www.zoho.com/us/inventory/pricing/); [Stockpile](https://www.thecanvus.com/)). Across 11 tools with a published first paid price (Fishbowl's is a quote-led, third-party estimate), the middle price (the **median**) is about **$49 a month**, and it stays $49 if you leave Fishbowl out; among the six lightweight tools it is about $32. (I calculated these medians from the competitor notes.) So SydIN Standard costs **one-third to one-fifth** of mainstream rivals such as Zoho and Sortly, but about half of BoxHero's $20. Per item the gap closes. SydIN Pro costs the same as BoxHero for the same 1,000 items, with 10 depots instead of 3, and SydIN Standard ($0.036 per item) costs about twice BoxHero's per-item rate.

The cheapest serious rival inside Lebanon is Odoo. Its Inventory app is free, with unlimited users, if it is the only app installed ([Odoo](https://www.odoo.com/pricing)). According to a third-party tracker (Odoo's own regional page could not be checked), Lebanon is on Odoo's "Middle East" price list: about **$7.25 per user per month** for the full suite in the first year on yearly billing, then $8.95 ($9.10 billed monthly) ([oec.sh](https://oec.sh/odoo-pricing/lebanon)). Against Odoo, SydIN wins on simplicity, the phone-camera scanner, no consultant, and Whish or OMT payment. It does not win on price.

What rivals lock behind higher plans matters more than their prices. Sortly includes phone barcode scanning in **every plan, including Free**, and locks label creation instead ([Sortly help](https://help.sortly.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000737832-Scan-and-Connect-Existing-QR-Codes-or-Barcodes)). BoxHero's free plan gives **100 items with barcode scanning and Excel import and export** ([BoxHero plans](https://www.boxhero.io/docs/faq/billing/plans-and-pricing)). Not all rivals do this: inFlow sells its scanning app, Stockroom, separately from about $99 a month ([inFlow Stockroom](https://www.inflowinventory.com/software-pricing-stockroom)), and scanning tiers for Zoho, Square and Loyverse were not confirmed. Rivals mostly charge more for locations, users, purchase orders and labels. Square puts purchase orders in its $49-per-location plan ([Square](https://squareup.com/us/en/point-of-sale/retail/pricing)), and Zoho allows 1, 2, 4, 6 and 10 locations as plans go up ([Zoho](https://www.zoho.com/us/inventory/pricing/)). SydIN already gives purchase orders, receiving and 3 depots for $9, which is generous.

The Free plan is the one place SydIN is stricter than the market: 50 items and no scanner, against 100 items with scanning at Sortly and BoxHero. Two fixes fit the evidence. You can raise Free to 100 items, or keep 50 items and give new accounts a 30-day trial of the paid plan, which BoxHero does for every new account ([BoxHero plans](https://www.boxhero.io/docs/faq/billing/plans-and-pricing)). **Freemium** means a free plan that lasts forever; a **free trial** gives the paid plan for a short time. I recommend keeping Free as it is and offering the trial on request. Every shop that takes it then feels the scanner — the moment SydIN "clicks" — while the scanner stays the clearest reason to pay. When the trial ends, a shop that added, say, 150 items keeps all of them, but must pay $9 to keep scanning and to add more. If, after your first 50 trials, fewer than 1 in 10 shops pay, let Free use the scanner in Lookup mode and watch whether that helps.

A **conversion rate** is the share of free users who become paying customers. For **self-serve** freemium products (ones a customer can sign up for and buy online without talking to a salesperson), **3–5% within six months is "good" and 6–8% is "great"** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-free-to-paid-conversion)). Trials that do not ask for a card convert about **4–6%** at the median, while trials that require a card convert **25–35%** ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). SydIN cannot ask for a card, because payment is manual. But a founder who personally follows up looks like "sales-assisted" freemium. A secondary blog (Userpilot) says free plans where the founder follows up personally convert **10–15%** ([Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/freemium-saas/)). Its labels disagree with the original survey, so treat 10–15% as a hopeful number, not a proven one. You only get that higher rate if you message or visit each trial shop before day 30.

A **value metric** is the thing a price grows with: items, depots, or users. A 2018 ProfitWell study of 6,000+ companies found that firms pricing on a value metric grew nearly twice as fast as firms that only changed features between plans ([ProfitWell, now Paddle](https://paddle.com/studios/shows/profitwell-report/value-metric-benchmarks)). Pricing expert Patrick Campbell calls per-user pricing "lazy" unless users truly track value ([Antoine Buteau](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-patrick-campbell/)). SydIN's items-plus-depots metric matches Sortly and BoxHero, so keep it. Keep team seats generous (3 and 10 extra people besides the owner in the code), because small Lebanese shops often share one phone, and tight seat limits only lead to shared passwords. One question is still open: nobody has measured how many items a Lebanese phone-accessory shop carries. Covers and chargers for many phone models can pass 250 items fast, which would push most real shops to Pro. Record the item count of your first 20 shops before you change any limit.

Should Standard cost more than $9? The global numbers would support $12. Two regional facts are often read as arguments against it, but neither settles it. About **30% of subscription products in the Middle East cost $1–$10 a month** ([Wamda, 2020](https://www.wamda.com/2020/12/look-subscriptions-based-economy-middle-east)), but that survey covered 193 mostly consumer subscription companies, so it shows what sellers charged, not what Lebanese depots will pay. And MicroConf reports that founders with $1–$9 entry plans added about **$316 of new monthly revenue each month**, against about $1,073 for founders whose entry plan cost $1,000 or more ([MicroConf](https://microconf.com/founders/10k-100k-arr)). SydIN's $9 sits inside that lowest $1–$9 band. This is a correlation from MicroConf's own summary of its 2024 survey, not a controlled test, and it compares with $1,000-a-month business software, which is not SydIN's world; if anything it argues for a higher entry price. The case for keeping $9 at launch rests on Lebanese evidence instead: local cloud prices that start at $15, the war, the cash habit, and the $150 one-time comparison. Raise revenue per shop through yearly plans and setup fees now, and test $12 on new customers at the price review (see the Conclusion).

## Yearly prepay and a capped founding group beat monthly bills and lifetime deals

A typical yearly discount is **15–20%** ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/should-you-offer-discounts-for-annual-commitments-a-strategic-guide-for-saas-leaders)). SydIN's "two months free" is 16.7%, right in that band. BoxHero gives 10%, Zoho about 25%, and Sortly 20% (50% in the first year during a promotion) ([Sortly](https://www.sortly.com/pricing/)). Keep two months free. The bigger problem is that very small customers choose monthly: SaaStr's rule of thumb is that **80% or more of very small customers pay monthly** unless pushed ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/what-is-the-average-percentage-of-annual-vs-monthly-plan-sold-for-a-btob-saas-startup-targeting-the-sme-market/)). Paddle notes that even if only 10–20% choose yearly, the business gets its money earlier and keeps more of its customers ([Paddle](https://www.paddle.com/resources/annual-plans)). For SydIN the case is stronger, because each monthly payment is a manual transfer and a fresh chance to quit. So show the yearly price first, as "$7.50 a month, paid once a year", and treat monthly as the backup. If a shop cannot pay $90 at once, offer 6 months for $49 (Standard) or $99 (Pro). That middle option is my suggestion; no study has tested it.

For early customers, avoid lifetime deals. A **lifetime deal** means a customer pays once and uses the software forever. It brings fast cash, but you keep supporting and hosting users who never pay again ([Dodo Payments](https://dodopayments.com/blogs/lifetime-deals-saas-pros-cons)). "Lifetime" really means "the lifetime of the vendor" ([Deepak Gupta](https://guptadeepak.com/startup-offers/marketplaces/appsumo)), and deal sites such as AppSumo reportedly keep 50–70% of the money (an unverified claim by a competitor) ([GTM Directory](https://thegtmdirectory.com/tools/appsumo/alternatives)). Use **grandfathering** instead: early customers keep the price they joined at, while new customers pay the newer price ([GoCardless](https://gocardless.com/en-us/guides/posts/what-is-the-grandfather-clause)). SaaStr adds that raising prices on early customers is rarely worth the anger, because their money is small next to new customers' money ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/change-price-saas-product-without-upsetting-existing-customers)).

So the founding offer works like this. The first 20 paying shops pay yearly in advance: **$69 for Standard or $149 for Pro**, about 22–23% below the normal yearly price. The price stays as long as they renew without a gap. They get one free setup visit (the same as the paid setup below: importing their Excel file, photos of up to 20 top items and one hour of training; typing items in by hand is extra) and your WhatsApp for questions, answered within one working day. In return they give a testimonial, regular feedback, and permission to use their shop as an example. Close the offer at 20 shops or on a date you write on the offer now (for example 30 June 2027), whichever comes first. In the base scenario below, 15 shops join as founders, and the offer costs about **$390** in price discount plus about **$200** of setup fees given free, roughly **$590** in year one — a small price for 15 real stories. Do not offer "50% off forever" on monthly plans: it halves already-small income and still needs 12 transfers a year.

Do not spend time on $9 versus $10. **Charm pricing** means prices ending in 9. It works in clothing shops, but no clear test shows it matters for business software ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/faqs/does-ending-your-price-in-a-9-versus-a-0-for-example-49-vs-50-really-affect-saas-buyers-behavior-and-has-anyone-a-b-tested-charm-pricing-versus-clean-round-numbers-to-see-if-it-matters)). $10 is the most common software price, and prices ending in 9 and 0 are about equally common ([Toolradar](https://toolradar.com/reports/saas-price-points-2026)). Keep $9: it is already in the app, and it reads as "under ten dollars."

The setup fee is a guess, because no Lebanese vendor publishes one. Two anchors help. Lebanese accountants charge about **$500–$2,000** to set up accounting software ([Voxire](https://voxire.com/blog/accounting-software-lebanon-smes-2026/)), and inFlow's current pricing page charges a one-time **$499 onboarding package** (help getting a new customer set up), which inFlow calls mandatory ([inFlow](https://www.inflowinventory.com/software-pricing)). A SydIN setup at **$29–$49** — you import the shop's Excel sheet, add photos for up to 20 top items, set low-stock levels, and train staff for an hour — is cheap by both measures. It also makes the shop much more likely to keep using SydIN. If the shop has no Excel file and wants you to type items in, quote a separate price per 100 items before you start. Never begin unpaid data entry. Printing QR label sheets for a shop is a low-risk extra tied to the scanner. Do not buy scanners or label printers to resell: that ties up cash and makes you the repair desk. Item packs can wait until Pro shops actually reach 1,000 items; BoxHero's $10 a month per extra 1,000 items ([BoxHero](https://www.boxhero.io/docs/faq/billing)) is a fair model when that day comes.

## Outside Lebanon, sell to Gulf diaspora shops first, at the same dollar price

**Purchasing power** means what money buys in a country. Many software firms charge less in poorer countries; this is called **PPP pricing** (purchasing power parity). The Big Mac index for January 2026 shows the gaps: a Big Mac costs $6.12 in the US, $5.36 in Lebanon, $5.17 in the UAE, $5.07 in Saudi Arabia, and only $2.65 in Egypt ([Mappr](https://www.mappr.co/thematic-maps/big-mac-index/)). Odoo, the one rival with clear regional prices, charges the Gulf about **0.54 times** its US price and Egypt about **0.29 times** ([oec.sh Saudi Arabia](https://oec.sh/odoo-pricing/saudi-arabia); [oec.sh Egypt](https://oec.sh/odoo-pricing/egypt)). SydIN's $9 and $19 are already about 0.2–0.7 times Zoho's $29–$39, so the Gulf needs no discount. Odoo charges Egypt about 0.53 times its Gulf price, and the Big Mac ratio between Egypt and the UAE is 0.51, which is where the Egypt prices below land. Odoo puts Lebanon on the same cheap list as Egypt, so Lebanon's full price rests on local rivals charging $15 and up, not on purchasing power.

The Gulf pays the most. In the UAE, Wafeq's cheapest plan is AED 57–69 a month, about **$15.50–$19** ([Wafeq](https://help.wafeq.com/hc/en-sa/articles/22187364508700-What-are-your-pricing-plans-and-available-packages)). In Saudi Arabia, Qoyod starts at SAR 120, about **$32** ([Qoyod](https://www.qoyod.com/en/knowledge-base/qoyod-plans-and-pricing-subscription-tiers-features-add-ons/)); Daftra at SAR 75–99, about $20–26 ([Daftra](https://www.daftra.com/en/hub/accounting-software-prices)); and Rewaa at **SAR 275 a month, yearly only**, about $73 ([Snad](https://www.snad.io/en/blog/afdal-barnamaj-nuqat-bay-mahalat-tajzia-saudi)). But these are Arabic products that sell e-invoicing compliance. **E-invoicing** means invoices must be made in a government-approved electronic format. Saudi Arabia's next wave reaches firms with revenue above **SAR 187,500 (about $50,000) by 1 February 2027** (per secondary sources; not confirmed on zatca.gov.sa) ([Dariba](https://www.dariba.co/zatca-e-invoicing-wave-25/)), and Rewaa raised **$45 million** in December 2025 ([Wamda](https://www.wamda.com/en/2025/12/saudi-arabias-rewaa-closes-45-million-series-b)). The UAE is easier. English is the business language, and small firms (revenue under AED 50 million) start e-invoicing only on **1 July 2027** ([Tally Solutions](https://tallysolutions.com/mena/uae-vat/uae-e-invoicing-timeline-2026-2027-complete-phase-by-phase-implementation-guide/)). Money already flows from the Gulf into Whish: from the UAE through Careem Pay and from Saudi Arabia through Tiqmo ([Whish offers](https://www.whish.money/offers/0-cash-out-fees)).

So the first market outside Lebanon is **diaspora shops** (shops abroad run by Lebanese families) and other **Levantine-run** shops and depots (run by Lebanese, Syrian, Jordanian or Palestinian owners) **in the UAE**, reached through family and supplier contacts. The same community in Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Kuwait comes next, once you have checked the e-invoicing rules there with a local accountant. (No source counted these shops; Qatar and Kuwait were not researched in depth.) Pitch SydIN as "stock and depots on your phone, next to your invoicing app", not as an accounting replacement. Charge the same $9 and $19 for now. One price list is easier for a solo founder, and an English-only stock app that sits beside another app is worth less than an all-in-one Arabic tool. Once SydIN has Arabic, an invoicing partner and about 10 Gulf customers (year two at the earliest), raise prices for *new* open-market Gulf customers to about $12–15 and $25–29; diaspora referrals stay on the dollar list.

One caution. SydIN now has a sales and invoice feature with VAT. In Saudi Arabia, Jordan (where e-invoicing has applied to **every VAT-registered business since 1 April 2025** ([VATupdate](https://www.vatupdate.com/2025/07/17/jordans-e-invoicing-phase-2-mandatory-compliance-begins-april-1-2025/))) and Egypt ([VATupdate](https://www.vatupdate.com/2026/02/23/briefing-document-podcast-e-invoicing-e-reporting-in-egypt/)), a SydIN invoice is not a legal e-invoice. The same will apply in the UAE from 1 July 2027 for small firms. Sell SydIN there as stock control, and check the wording with a local accountant before you market it. Tell every Gulf customer in writing: "SydIN invoices are not official tax invoices. Keep using your invoicing app for those." This is not legal advice.

Elsewhere, collecting money is the real wall, not price. Egyptian banks cut foreign card spending to **as little as $50 a month** for ordinary customers in 2022; banks eased somewhat in August 2025 (FX fees fell from 5% to 3%, and some banks such as AAIB raised caps to $10,000), but limits still vary by bank and customer ([Digital Boom](https://adigitalboom.com/market-watch/egypt-foreign-currency-card-limits/)). Only about **11% of Iraqis** have a formal bank account, although card and wallet access exceeds 40%, driven by government salary payments ([Better Than Cash Alliance](https://www.betterthancash.org/news/access-isnt-enough-the-reality-of-digital-payments-in-the-arab-region)). Syria's card networks are not confirmed working, even after the US repealed the Caesar Act in December 2025 ([Global Finance](https://gfmag.com/economics-policy-regulation/with-lifting-of-us-sanctions-syria-banks-reconnect/)). And **77% of Moroccan small firms** accept no digital payments at all ([Mastercard](https://www.mastercard.com/news/eemea/en/newsroom/press-releases/en/2025-1/february/mastercard-sme-confidence-index-morocco-holds-potential-for-digital-economic-transformation/)). A **merchant of record** — a company such as Paddle that sells on your behalf, collects card payments and handles tax, then pays you — could open card payments in the Gulf. Paddle sells to 200+ countries ([Paddle](https://developer.paddle.com/concepts/sell/supported-countries-locales/)), but nobody has confirmed it will pay out to a seller living in Lebanon. Paddle reports, without publishing its method, that enabling local payment methods lifted checkout conversion (the share of people on the payment page who actually pay) from **4.3% to 6.5%** ([Paddle](https://www.paddle.com/blog/local-payment-methods-fltr)). That suggests how a customer can pay may matter as much as the price; no study here compared the two.

The simplest structure for a solo founder is a few price groups, not a price for every country. Manual payment makes this enforceable, because the way a customer pays (Whish, an Iraqi wallet, an Egyptian transfer) shows where they are. Group C prices are the outside-markets research's suggestions, set against local rivals such as Daftra Egypt and Bumpa. Group B prices (Jordan, Iraq) have no local price data behind them: they rest only on an assumed 0.75× multiplier, because no Jordanian or Iraqi inventory or POS price was found, so treat them as a placeholder guess. None of these prices has been tested. **Only row A applies to you this year.** Rows B and C are for reference only. Do not publish these prices or sell in these countries until SydIN has Arabic, a way to collect local money, and at least 50 paying customers.

| **A: dollar list** | Lebanon; Lebanese- and Levantine-run shops in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait; diaspora paying by Whish or USDT | **$9 / $19** ($90 / $190 a year) | Lebanon now. West Africa deserves a note. About **100,000 Lebanese live in Côte d'Ivoire and 30,000 in Nigeria**, many in trade ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people_in_West_Africa)). If a contact there asks, they can pay the normal dollar price by USDT. Do not run pilots or translate SydIN into French this year. In Nigeria, the bookkeeping app Kippa shut down in January 2024 and users lost their stock data ([TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2024/02/23/kippa-users-left-in-the-dark/)), so "export your data anytime" is a real selling point.

## A realistic first year: 5 to 40 new paying shops, $450 to $3,600 collected

Three more terms. **MRR** (monthly recurring revenue) is the subscription money that arrives each month; a yearly payment counts as one-twelfth per month. **ARR** is MRR times 12. **Churn** is the share of paying customers who leave each month.

The benchmarks are sobering. Among indie founders who *reached* $1,000 MRR, the median took **8 months**, but that sample counts only the winners ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/it-takes-5-months-to-reach-1k-in-mrr-491742f806)). In MicroConf's 2024 survey (data from late 2023) of about 700 independent software companies, the largest single group (17.7%) earned **under $1,000 MRR**, and about 30% were flat or shrinking ([MicroConf](https://microconf.com/saas-this-year)). Small-business customers churn at **3–7% a month** ([Kalungi](https://kalungi.com/blog/saas-churn-rate-benchmarks)), and businesses with under 10 employees at about **8.9% a month** ([Orb](https://www.withorb.com/blog/saas-churn-statistics)). At 5% a month, the average customer stays about 20 months; at 9%, about 11. At SydIN's list prices, the average paying shop brings about **$10.50–$11 a month**, so **$1,000 MRR needs about 90–100 paying shops**. That is not a realistic year-one target.

I built three scenarios with a simple model. Month 1 is the month the live site opens. Its assumptions: 3 of every 4 paying shops choose Standard. The first 20 take the founding yearly price. After that, half pay yearly at list price and half pay monthly. Monthly payers churn 9%, 7% or 5% a month (weak, base, strong). Yearly payers do not leave within the first year, because they have already paid. One in three non-founding shops buys a $39 setup. New paying shops arrive at an even pace through the year. The model assumes all of the first 20 paying shops prepay a year; benchmarks say only 10–20% of small customers choose yearly. Each scenario starts from the low end of its benchmark band in the research notes (5–8, 15–25 and 40–60 shops), because of the 2026 war and your small online following.

The 3-in-4 Standard split is a guess, and it conflicts with the item-count warning above. If most depots carry more than 250 items, most will need Pro. Each shop would then bring in more money, but fewer may agree to pay, because $190 a year is a bigger ask than $90. Re-run these numbers once you know the item counts of your first 10 depots.

| Scenario | New paying shops in 12 months | Paying shops at month 12 | MRR at month 12 | Money collected in year one | What has to be true |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Weak** | 5 | 5 | about $37 | about $450 | Mostly friends and family; no repeatable way to find shops |
| **Base** | 15 | about 15 | about $110 | about $1,300 | You visit and set up shops yourself, about one new paying shop a month; 15 of the Founding 20 places are taken, and every one of those shops prepays a year |
| **Strong** | 40 | about 39 | about $350 | about $3,600 | A supplier, importer or trade group sends you shops, about 3 new paying shops a month; Founding 20 fills by month 6 |

In the weak and base cases every paying shop is a founding yearly payer, so no shop leaves in year one; the churn rate only matters at renewal. Across all three scenarios, churn barely affects year one because almost every payer prepays. If only 1 in 5 paying shops prepays, as the benchmarks suggest, the base case ends year one with about 11 shops, about $118 MRR and about $1,125 collected, and only about 3 shops take a Founding 20 place, so the offer would not fill in year one.

These are amounts collected, before costs. Once you have a paying customer, Vercel Pro costs about $20 a month ($240 a year). Supabase Pro, if you add it for backups, costs about $25 a month ($300 a year). Whish also takes 1% when you cash out (3% if a shop pays by payment link). After those costs, the weak case runs from a loss of about $100 to a gain of about $200, the base case leaves about $780–$1,080, and the strong case about $2,990–$3,290. The strong case also collects more than the $3,000 a personal Whish account can hold, so open a Whish Business account before collections pass about $2,500.

The base case's ARR is about $1,300, the same as its money collected, because every base-case shop is a founder who prepaid a year. "Money collected" includes yearly payments received in advance, so part of it pays for months after month 12. All of the base MRR is prepaid yearly money that has not yet been renewed. If renewals match a vendor-cited annual-plan benchmark (92% kept), about $9 of it is lost in year two; if they match micro-business churn (8.9% a month), about $75 is lost. Treat month-12 MRR as an upper bound until the first renewals come in. For scale, the base case collects about 4 months of Lebanon's minimum wage in a year, and the strong case about 11 months. After hosting costs, that is pocket money for a student, not a salary.

The funnel (the path from a free sign-up to a paying customer) explains why your time decides the outcome. To get the base case's 15 paying shops, you need **300–500 active free accounts** at 3–5%, 250–375 at the 4–6% median for trials without a card, or **100–150** with personal follow-up, if that hopeful 10–15% holds. Even the best case means 8–13 new trial shops every month. With a tiny online following, that means visiting shops, joining shop owners' WhatsApp groups, and getting introductions from suppliers. Churn means you must keep finding new shops just to stay the same size: once you have about 20 monthly customers, you lose about one a month at 5% churn, so you need more than one new paying shop a month just to stand still, and 2–3 a month grows the base by 1–2. In these scenarios this bites in year two, when the yearly founders come up for renewal. What pushes you toward the strong case is one channel that sends many shops at once, such as a phone-accessory importer whose retailers all use SydIN. What pushes you toward the weak case is the 2026 contraction, shop closures, and weeks when exams leave no time for visits. Every number here depends on how many hours you spend with shops, not on how big the market is, and every benchmark behind it comes from US and European software sold by card.

## Conclusion

This research changes the question you should be asking. SydIN is not overpriced anywhere it can sell today, and it is probably underpriced in the Gulf. But at 5 to 40 customers, the price tag barely moves the total. What moves it is the shape of payment and the hours spent with shops. A yearly plan framed as "pay once for the year" turns the Lebanese habit of one-time software purchases from an obstacle into a sales line, and it removes eleven manual transfers per customer. The founding offer matters less for its revenue than for the stories it buys. Those stories are the key to the Gulf diaspora: the one nearby market that pays more, already sends money into Whish, and does business in the English SydIN already has.

Treat year one as a test of four questions, each cheap to answer. How many items do real shops carry, which decides whether Standard or Pro is the true entry plan? Do owners prefer yearly or 6-month payments? Does Whish Business offer automatic collection, or a business account that fits SydIN? And will a merchant of record such as Paddle pay out to a seller in Lebanon, which would open card payments in the Gulf? Review prices after 20 shops have paid the list price (not the founding price), or 12 months after launch, whichever comes first, but do not raise Lebanese prices before 2027. Until then, record how many trial shops decline to pay and why. If almost nobody argues about $9, raise the price for new customers (test $12) and let the founders keep theirs.
